It starts with
an ordinary school day.

A school bag is useful because it keeps learning materials together. But it also has mass, takes up space, and must be lifted and carried from place to place.

Imagine leaving home with notebooks, paper, books, a water bottle, and a few things that might be needed later. One item may seem light on its own; several items together create a load that has to be moved every morning, through hallways, up stairs, and between classrooms.

That everyday experience led us to ask: how do students describe their bag contents, carrying habits, and discomfort—and what can physics help us understand about the mechanics involved?

Our questionnaire gathered self-reported experiences and practices. It is a useful starting point, but it is not the same as directly measuring bag mass, posture, spinal loading, or injury. We keep that distinction clear throughout this website.

“A survey can tell us what students report. Physics helps us identify what could be measured next.”THE IDEA GUIDING THIS PROJECT
A / THE LOAD

What goes in?

Notebooks and paper were the most frequently reported bag contents. Some respondents also said they sometimes brought materials they did not use that day.

B / THE MOVEMENT

How is it carried?

Bag position, strap adjustment, and lifting technique change how a load is held and moved. These are observations, not direct measurements of spinal force.

C / THE EXPERIENCE

What do students notice?

Respondents reported shoulder discomfort and difficulty walking with their bags. The survey alone cannot prove that the bag caused these experiences.

Everyday carrying
is mechanics in motion.

These concepts explain physical relationships. The drawings are conceptual illustrations, not measurements of a student’s spine.

CONCEPT 01

Weight & force

Mass tells us how much matter is in a bag. Weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass. A heavier bag has greater weight under the same gravity.

F = mg
Force = mass × gravitational acceleration
🎒↓ weight
CONCEPT 02

Torque & posture

Torque describes a turning effect. If the load acts farther from a selected joint, the moment arm can be larger and the turning effect may increase.

τ = rF⊥
Torque = moment arm × perpendicular force
●━━━━▲moment arm
CONCEPT 03

Work & lifting

When a bag is lifted vertically, work is done against gravity. If mass and lifting height are known, the work can be estimated.

W = mgh
Work = mass × gravity × height
↑ lifting height (h)
Important distinction: these equations cannot calculate spinal-disc compression or diagnose injury from survey percentages. That would require suitable measurements and validated biomechanical methods.

READING THE RESULTS

What might happen between packing and carrying?

Notebooks and paper were both the most frequently carried materials and the item category most often identified as contributing to bag weight (34.6%). Meanwhile, 38.5% said they sometimes brought materials they did not use, and 57.7% gave “may need them later” as a reason for carrying unused materials.

These answers suggest a practical question: could checking the daily class schedule help identify materials that can safely stay at home or in classroom storage? The survey does not establish how much weight would be removed, so a next step would be to weigh bags before and after a packing change.

BAG POSITION

Distance matters in torque

34.6% reported carrying bags low on their backs; 23.1% said their bags hung away from their backs. In mechanics, distance from a pivot can affect torque, but the survey did not measure actual torque.

LIFTING

Technique is worth exploring

30.8% reported bending their knees and lifting close to the body. Another 26.9% reported bending mainly at the waist and 26.9% lifting with one hand. These are self-reported habits.

MOBILITY

Discomfort deserves context

30.8% reported sometimes experiencing discomfort or difficulty. For school mobility, 34.6% reported a significant effect and another 34.6% a moderate effect. The survey cannot establish cause.

Research accuracy noteThe methodology refers to 25 respondents, while several percentages are consistent with 26 responses. Confirm the final sample size and all percentages against the original survey before submission.

Start with a small change.
Then measure it.

The proposed School Bag Load Management and Carrying System combines practical ideas to try and evaluate. They are recommendations, not outcomes already proven by the questionnaire.

01 / PACKING

Pack for the timetable

Check the class schedule and bring the materials needed for the day. Decide whether “might need it later” means an item must be carried all day.

02 / STORAGE

Explore secure storage

Schools could consider classroom storage or lockers for supplies not needed in every class, while considering access, safety, and space.

03 / HANDLING

Adjust and lift mindfully

Keep the bag close to the body, adjust straps for a stable fit, and lift with the load close rather than reaching far away. Seek qualified help for persistent pain.

04 / EVALUATION

Measure the change

Weigh bags before and after packing changes under consistent conditions. If feasible, record lifting height and time to estimate work and average power.

A NEXT STEP FOR THE RESEARCH

Could the bag become lighter after a checklist?

Record bag mass before the intervention, use the checklist or storage plan, then measure again. Compare results transparently. Do not claim spinal-load reduction or injury prevention unless suitable evidence supports it.

Listening first.
Measuring next.

The report describes a questionnaire-based study of Senior High School students in public schools around Iligan City. Responses were summarized using frequencies, percentages, tables, and graphs.

01

Ask about daily routines

The Google Forms questionnaire asked about bag types, contents, carrying and lifting practices, reported discomfort, and willingness to try interventions.

02

Look for patterns

Responses were organized into summary statistics. These describe what participants reported but cannot show that one factor caused another.

03

Design a testable response

The report proposes packing checklists, classroom storage, and handling practices. A follow-up could measure bag mass before and after changes.

Limitation: the report contains self-reported answers, not direct measurements of bag mass, spinal loading, disc compression, posture angles, or injury risk.
